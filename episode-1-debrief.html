Tactical stability, but no breakthroughs: my assessment of the Trump-Xi summit, and what comes next.

The Trump-Xi summit: tactical stability, but no breakthroughs

BY TREVOR H. RUDOLPH  |  OCTOBER 5, 2026

A summit should be judged against the structural problems it was meant to address, not its staging. On the premiere of Memo: Random, my guest, Naomi Wilson of ITI, and I assessed last month’s Washington meeting on that basis, and I graded the president against five criteria central to the bilateral relationship.

Assessing the outcomes

Criterion and assessment Grade
1. Chinese subsidies to critical industries. Industrial policy in green technology and autos drives overcapacity that distorts global markets. This issue was not raised, leaving a key structural driver of trade friction untouched. Unsatisfactory
2. Intellectual property theft. Once a pillar of U.S. China policy, now largely absent from administration messaging. Its absence signals deprioritization, not resolution. Unsatisfactory
3. The trade truce. Reciprocal leverage: U.S. chip export controls against Chinese minerals controls. Extension preserves the equilibrium, but only until January 2027, deferring the disputes beneath it. Needs work
4. AI risk. A dialogue covering AI incidents, starting in November, is a credible confidence-building measure. Without deliverables, its value is procedural. Encouraging; needs fleshing out
5. Taiwan. Little was said publicly, but continued approval of arms sales is substantive continuity. Some credit

Final Grade for U.S. side: C-

Compliance, ambiguity, and the AI question

My guest offered her perspective that diplomacy is more art than science, and both delegations merit more than passing marks. Washington’s complaint that Beijing underdelivers on critical minerals is often well founded, yet “compliance” is not binary, since licensing procedures, provincial authorities, and national security reviews pull against one another. Future agreements should therefore specify verifiable, process-level metrics. She also viewed the fact sheet’s omission of Taiwan as deliberate and prudent. On AI, the premise that both governments race symmetrically toward artificial general intelligence (AGI) may be mistaken: Beijing emphasizes diffusing AI across society, so any U.S.-China dialogue should focus on confidence-building, not a shared finish line.

What to watch for next

1. Institutionalized dialogue. The goal should be to sustain the AI talks and broader crisis communication to avoid misunderstandings.
2. Human control over nuclear launch decisions. A commitment that only humans make final launch decisions, plus attention to loss-of-control risk, would build on an understanding the prior U.S. administration reached.
3. Pressure on subsidies. Washington should press harder on state support for key Chinese industries and its costs to manufacturers elsewhere.
4. Critical minerals diversification. Diversifying supply increases U.S. leverage and reduces dependency. Progress is decent but needs further investment.

Next stop: Shenzhen

The truce lapses in early January, which makes the next two leader-level encounters decisive: the APEC Leaders’ Meeting in Shenzhen on November 18–19, to which President Trump is expected to travel weeks after the U.S. midterms, and the G20 in Miami in December. The stakes are whether the leaders convert a tactical pause into durable, verifiable commitments on subsidies, export controls, and AI, or simply roll the arrangement over with the hardest questions deferred again. It remains to be seen which path Shenzhen reveals.
