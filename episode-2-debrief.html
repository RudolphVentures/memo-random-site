How to regulate AI: gaps in rules and governance

BY TREVOR H. RUDOLPH  |  NOVEMBER 21, 2026

Artificial intelligence (AI) promises a great deal, but the more we gain, the more we stand to lose, especially if we lose control. I opened Episode 2 of Memo: Random with three risks: misuse, loss of control, and labor market disruption. I then asked my guests, Taylor Roberts of Zenity and Konstantinos Komaitis of the Atlantic Council, whether regulation is the answer. In short, it is necessary but will not work without governance, and both have gaps.

What we learned

On the rules, neither guest could point to a model law. Roberts noted that the European Union (EU) AI Act never mentions AI agents and will be costly to implement, yet he called leaving the matter to companies “a recipe for disaster.” Doing nothing is not the answer. His written work explains part of the difficulty: “AI regulation” is several debates at once, and each requires a different instrument. He would focus first on security, which he called “a core one” and “somewhat of a bipartisan issue.”

On governance, Komaitis was direct: “the most fundamental problem is not the lack of regulation but it’s the lack of governance.” In his view, we conflate the two: regulation is the rules, governance is the institutions that implement and enforce them, which most governments lack. He also wants liability and consumer protection rules, noting that China, its authoritarian context aside, is concrete on both.

We disagreed most on jobs. Komaitis called labor disruption “the most immediate systemic issue” and was surprised that it receives so little regulatory attention. I agree. Roberts is waiting for more evidence, and the evidence so far supports part of each view: the latest Stanford data show no economy-wide displacement, but employment for workers aged 22 to 25 in the most AI-exposed occupations has fallen about 19% behind peers in less-exposed fields, mainly through reduced hiring. The effect appears real but concentrated at the entry level, which is where policy should look first.

Accountability, not autonomy

This summer’s Hugging Face breach, in which about 700 OpenAI agents from a cyber evaluation attacked the company, set off a wave of “rogue AI” headlines. Both guests rejected the label. Roberts noted that guardrails exist and “were not in place.” Komaitis stressed that these systems are “created by humans,” as he has written elsewhere. “Rogue,” Roberts argued, implies that nothing could have been done, which shifts responsibility from those deploying agents to the technology.

This matters because “loss of control” incidents are accelerating: the Centre for Long-Term Resilience recorded a 4.9-fold rise in monthly “scheming” incidents from October 2025 to March 2026. Komaitis wants liability provisions that make developers “think twice.” Roberts suggested “a liability regime or at the very least a due diligence regime,” informed by security firms and insurers, not only model providers.

Governance, not just regulation

Komaitis opposes a single global authority, such as an “International Atomic Energy Agency for AI.” His alternative draws on the internet, which scaled because separate bodies handled standards, addresses, and domain names, and coordinated. For AI, he would empower the safety and trust institutes that already exist, such as the United Kingdom’s AI Security Institute, the U.S. Center for AI Standards and Innovation, and their peers in the ten-member International Network for Advanced AI Measurement, Evaluation and Science, to share evaluations, incident reports, and risk assessments. They need not agree on AI’s future, only on the facts.

Roberts’s own mapping counts 18 AI security initiatives, seven of them working on agentic AI with little visible coordination, while consensus-based bodies such as the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) are inclusive but slow. If the coalitions agreed on common practices, standards bodies could start from that draft, not from a blank page.

This week in Shenzhen

Presidents Trump and Xi met in Shenzhen on November 19, on the sidelines of the APEC leaders’ meeting. According to the Chinese readout, Xi called for closer coordination on AI security risks and for keeping AI “under human control.” Trump told the summit that the U.S. opposes “globalist” regulation of AI. The readout cites no AI-specific agreement. As I said on the show, no grand bargain is needed for progress; the fact that the two governments are discussing AI risk is a win in the short term. My guests cautioned against nuclear and arms-race analogies: Komaitis because AI development, unlike nuclear infrastructure, cannot be seen or verified, and Roberts because no one can define what winning the race would mean. Both saw autonomous weapons as a place to start. It remains to be seen whether Shenzhen produces more than just statements.

Three themes stood out. First, the audience wants binding rules with international reach. Two-thirds backed a binding international framework, as did I, while U.S.-style light-touch rules drew 4.8%. In practice, this would mean treaty obligations and a body able to verify compliance. The closest example is the Council of Europe’s Framework Convention on AI, the first legally binding AI treaty, which the U.S. signed under the Biden administration in 2024 but, as of September, only the EU had ratified. The closest proposal is the Finland–Norway Call for Control of Frontier AI Models, backed by 34 leaders from 32 countries, which seeks mandatory testing and international institutions to set and verify standards. This is the opposite of the president’s stance in Shenzhen. Viewers want enforceable global rules, whereas my guests worry about who would run them.

Second, viewers were confident individually but supportive collectively. Three in four said they are not worried about losing their job to AI, yet 47.1% backed a universal basic income paid for by AI companies and another 23.5% a progressive tax on token usage. About seven in ten want AI’s gains to help fund a safety net, which suggests people see the risk as social, not personal.

Third, viewers were pessimistic about Washington but hopeful about diplomacy. Only 17.6% expect federal AI legislation after the midterms, yet 69.2% want the U.S. and China to coordinate on AI risk.

How to close the gaps

Building on my guests’ proposals, here is where I would begin on AI governance and regulation.

1.Distributed governance built on existing institutes. Governments should fund and connect the institutes named above, with common standards for evaluation and incident reporting, so that, as Komaitis puts it, an evaluation in London is comparable with one in Nairobi.
2.A shared starting point for standards. Industry coalitions such as the Coalition for Secure AI and the OWASP Gen AI Security Project should publish common practices for securing AI agents that standards bodies such as NIST can adopt as a first draft.
3.Accountability through security rules first. Congress should hear from security firms and insurers and set a security due-diligence standard for deploying agents (access limits, monitoring, incident reporting). New AI obligations should align with existing cyber rules such as NIS2 and the Cyber Resilience Act.
4.Labor as a policy priority. Governments should track early-career hiring and invest in reskilling, which Komaitis called their responsibility. Our audience wants AI companies to help pay.
5.Narrow U.S.–China agreements. Washington and Beijing should start with addressing catastrophic uses of AI, such as autonomous weapons, where both guests saw common ground.
